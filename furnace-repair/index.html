A modern furnace runs a strict startup sequence, and knowing where the sequence breaks is most of the diagnosis. The thermostat calls, the inducer motor spins up to prove draft through a pressure switch, the hot surface igniter glows, the gas valve opens, the burners light, the flame sensor confirms fire, and only then does the blower deliver heat to the house. In Olathe, KS, the failures cluster at predictable points: an oxide-coated flame sensor, a hot surface igniter cracked from years of thermal cycling, a pressure switch that will not close because the inducer is weak or the condensate trap on a high-efficiency model has clogged, or a control board with a burned relay. Each one stops the sequence cold, and each one mimics the others from the hallway.

Rather than guess, Olathe HVAC tests the sequence one stage at a time. We read igniter resistance, measure flame signal in microamps, check that the pressure switch responds to real inducer draft, confirm voltage at the gas valve, and test the blower and its capacitor. Because this is a fuel-burning appliance, the safety basics come with every visit: we inspect the heat exchanger, leak-test the gas connections, and verify flue draft. You cannot tell a furnace problem from a furnace hazard by looking at the thermostat. Before we leave, temperature rise is measured against the data plate so airflow and flame are matched as the manufacturer intended.

Conventional or condensing, gas, propane, or electric, we work on them all, across every major brand, including the traps, drains, and dual pressure switches that come with high-efficiency models. The parts most likely to fail are stocked on every truck: igniters, flame sensors, pressure switches, capacitors, inducer assemblies, and blower motors. Signs You Need Furnace Repair in Olathe

The sooner you notice these red flags, the easier and cheaper the problem is to resolve. Watch for these telltale signs that professional help is needed:

  • Grinding or high-pitched squeals from the blower
  • A fan that keeps running while the registers blow cool air
  • Water, rust stains, or black soot around the flue or cabinet
  • A bang or boom when the burners ignite late
  • A flame that burns soft and yellow rather than steady blue
  • Burners light, run a few seconds, then shut down repeatedly
  • Short cycling every few minutes without reaching the setpoint
  • The igniter glows and clicks, yet no flame ever appears

Why Professional Furnace Repair Matters

Gas, open flame, high voltage, and a flue all share one cabinet in a furnace, and the most dangerous failure is the one you cannot see: a damaged heat exchanger that lets combustion gases into the air the blower sends through your home. Every morning that delayed ignition keeps booming at startup, that exchanger takes more stress. The diagnostic side has its own pitfalls. When a furnace trips its high limit, the switch itself may be bad, or the furnace may be protecting itself from a clogged filter or a failing blower. Replacing the switch when the real issue is airflow removes a safety device and leaves the actual problem in place.

Our techs look at every symptom in the context of the whole furnace. We measure flame signal instead of assuming it, which keeps us from replacing a healthy sensor while a cracked igniter or weak gas valve goes unnoticed. Draft is confirmed at the pressure switch and at the flue. Temperature rise is checked against the data plate, catching airflow problems before they shorten the life of the heat exchanger. The gas side is leak-tested with instruments, never with a quick sniff. The result is a furnace proven safe over a full cycle, plus an honest opinion if the unit is getting close to the point where replacing it beats repairing it again.

Furnace Repair Prevention Tips

A little routine maintenance goes a long way toward avoiding a mid-season breakdown. Start with these practical measures our team relies on:

  • Replace the filter every month while the furnace is working hard, since it costs less than any other part
  • Keep storage, lint, and especially chlorine-based products away from the furnace closet
  • Avoid packing the utility room, because burners need open space for combustion air
  • Notice how the furnace sounds at startup, because a quiet light-off is healthy and a boom is not
  • Have the furnace checked every autumn, while a weak igniter or fouled sensor is still an easy fix
  • Keep the condensate trap and drain clear on high-efficiency units to prevent lockouts
  • Put a carbon monoxide detector on every level and test them monthly
